## 3. Biblical Reading Sequence

Rather than attempting to read the Bible cover-to-cover initially, I recommend this progression:

1. **Gospel of Mark** - The shortest gospel; provides narrative framework for Jesus's ministry
2. **Genesis 1-11** - Creation narratives and primeval history
3. **Exodus 1-24** - Foundational covenant relationship
4. **Psalms** (selections) - Poetic expressions of faith experience
5. **Amos** - Prophetic critique of social injustice
6. **Romans** - Paul's systematic theological argument
7. **Job** - Wrestling with suffering and divine justice
8. **John** - Theological reflection on Jesus's identity and mission

This sequence balances narrative accessibility, theological depth, and genre diversity while providing foundations for further study.

## 6. Critical Thinking Questions for Biblical Sections

### Torah/Pentateuch
1. What patterns in creation narratives reveal about ancient cosmology?
2. How do covenant structures reflect ancient Near Eastern treaty formats?
3. What theological purposes do genealogies serve?
4. How do legal codes balance ideal society with practical governance?

### Historical Books
1. How do narrative techniques shape theological interpretations of history?
2. What criteria for leadership emerge across different historical periods?
3. How is causality (divine and human) presented in historical events?
4. What patterns of societal flourishing and decline are presented?

### Wisdom Literature
1. How do different wisdom traditions address epistemological questions?
2. What tensions exist between idealized wisdom and lived experience?
3. How do poetic forms convey theological content differently than prose?
4. What underlying assumptions about human nature inform wisdom texts?

### Prophetic Literature
1. How do prophets reinterpret earlier traditions for new contexts?
2. What rhetorical strategies do prophets employ to communicate divine messages?
3. How do prophetic critiques reflect specific historical circumstances?
4. What tension exists between judgment and hope in prophetic literature?

### Gospels
1. How do narrative structures frame theological claims about Jesus?
2. What textual variations between parallel accounts reveal about authorial intent?
3. How do parables function as both revealing and concealing devices?
4. What implicit theological claims underlie miracle accounts?

### Pauline Epistles
1. How does Paul's argumentation reflect both Jewish and Hellenistic reasoning?
2. What core theological concepts provide the foundation for ethical instructions?
3. How do Paul's situational responses reveal systematic theological frameworks?
4. What metaphorical systems does Paul employ for theological communication?

### Apocalyptic Literature
1. How do symbolic systems function as both concealing and revealing devices?
2. What historical circumstances inform apocalyptic worldviews?
3. How do temporal frameworks (linear vs. cyclical) operate in apocalyptic texts?
4. What epistemological claims underlie visionary experiences?

## Theological Glossary

**Exegesis** - The critical interpretation of biblical texts, focused on extracting meaning from the text rather than importing meaning into it

**Hermeneutics** - The theory and methodology of text interpretation, particularly biblical texts

**Soteriology** - The theological study of salvation, its means, and effects

**Eschatology** - The study of ultimate destiny, both individual (death, afterlife) and cosmic (end times)

**Canon** - The officially recognized texts that constitute authoritative scripture

**Theodicy** - Theological attempts to reconcile divine goodness with the existence of evil

**Ecclesiology** - Theological study of the nature and structure of the church

**Pneumatology** - The study of the Holy Spirit, spiritual gifts, and spiritual operation

**Christology** - Theological study of the person and nature of Jesus Christ

**Anthropology** - In theology, the study of human nature, purpose, and destiny

## Starting Point: The Gospel of Mark

### Historical Context
Mark's Gospel was likely the earliest written gospel (c. 65-75 CE), composed during or shortly after the Jewish-Roman War and Nero's persecution. Written for a primarily Gentile audience experiencing persecution, it emphasizes Jesus as suffering servant rather than triumphant king. Its compact, action-oriented narrative reflects the urgency of its message in a time of crisis, while its abrupt ending (if original) highlights the ongoing nature of discipleship.

### Key Themes
- Jesus as the suffering Messiah
- Discipleship as costly following
- The "messianic secret" (Jesus concealing his identity)
- Kingdom of God as present yet not fully realized
- Conflict with religious and political authorities
- Apocalyptic expectation

### Primary Interpretive Challenges
1. The "Messianic Secret" - Why Jesus repeatedly commands silence about his identity
2. The abrupt ending at 16:8 vs. later additions
3. The portrayal of disciples as consistently misunderstanding Jesus
4. The relationship between apocalyptic predictions and historical events
5. The theological significance of Jesus's death in Mark's account

### Recommended Approach for First Reading
1. Read the entire gospel in one sitting (takes approximately 1-2 hours)
2. Create a structural outline identifying major sections
3. Track recurring words and phrases (e.g., "immediately," "kingdom of God")
4. Map the geographical movement throughout the narrative
5. Note patterns in how Jesus interacts with different groups

### Reflection Questions
1. How does Mark's portrayal of Jesus challenge conventional expectations?
2. What patterns do you notice in how discipleship is portrayed?
3. How does the narrative pace affect your understanding of the message?
4. What systems of authority are challenged in this gospel?
5. How might the original audience have received this message during a time of persecution?
